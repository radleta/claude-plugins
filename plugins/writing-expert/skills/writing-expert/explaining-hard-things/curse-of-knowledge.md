# Curse of Knowledge: Diagnosing and Counteracting the Expert's Blind Spot

Steven Pinker, in *The Sense of Style* (2014), argues that the curse of knowledge is the single most pervasive cause of bad explanatory writing. The problem is not that experts write poorly — it is that knowing something makes it cognitively very hard to reconstruct what it was like not to know it. Experts unconsciously skip steps, use jargon as shorthand for concepts that took them months to build, and assume the reader shares a mental model that only years of immersion can produce.

## The Core Mechanics

When you know something, that knowledge restructures your mental representation of the domain — connections fire automatically, definitions are implicit, causal chains feel obvious. A reader who lacks that structure experiences your "obvious" explanation as a sequence of unlabeled leaps. They don't know which parts are prerequisites and which are conclusions. They don't know what is being defined versus what is being assumed.

The curse has two diagnostic signals:
1. **Jargon without definition** — using a term before the concept it names is anchored in an example or image the reader can hold.
2. **Skipped inference steps** — moving from A to C because "anyone who knows B would see this," when the reader has not been given B.

## Countermeasures

**Find the first unknown step.** Re-read your explanation and ask: at what point does the reader first encounter a term or a concept that is not defined in the text or in shared common knowledge? That is your entry point for revision. Do not patch later confusions without fixing the first gap — readers who lose the thread rarely recover.

**Reconstruct the concept from first principles, then compress.** Write the full derivation (even if it's twice as long as needed), then cut back to the minimum path that a reader with no prior knowledge could still follow. This builds the compressed explanation from the right direction — you know what steps can be trusted to inference and what must be stated.

**Use a naive test reader.** The most reliable cure: have someone who is not in your field read a draft and mark every sentence where they lose the thread. Their confusion is data about your skipped steps, not about their intelligence.

**Translate jargon into images before using the term.** Introduce the concept through a concrete example or image first. Name it second. The sequence is: here is the thing → here is what it's called. Reversing this order (name first, then explain) triggers the curse because the reader has no hook for the name.

**Self-check: can you state the concept in one sentence that a curious twelve-year-old would understand?** Not because the final explanation needs to be that simple, but because this test forces you to find the kernel of the idea before you elaborate. If you can't state the kernel, you haven't fully understood it yourself — and the curse will propagate into your prose.

## What This Is Not

The curse of knowledge is not about dumbing things down. Pinker is clear that the goal is not to write for the lowest common denominator but to find the **minimum prior knowledge a reader actually needs** and then build explicitly from there. The sophisticated reader who already knows the prerequisite skips that scaffolding easily; the reader who needs it cannot build it from omitted steps.

The cure is not condescension — it is precision about what you are assuming.

## How to apply

1. **Before drafting:** list the three to five concepts a reader must already hold to follow your explanation. If those concepts are not in their vocabulary, either define them first or redesign your explanation to not require them.
2. **During drafting:** after every paragraph, ask "what would a reader who has not yet read the next paragraph think this sentence means?" If the answer is wrong (because the next paragraph redefines it), restructure.
3. **During revision:** find the first sentence a naive reader would stumble on. Fix that sentence, then propagate the fix forward. Do not patch later symptoms without curing the first cause.
4. **For jargon:** introduce every technical term via example before you name it. Order is: concrete case → name. Never name → define → abstract.
5. **Self-audit test:** write a one-sentence plain-language summary of the core idea. If you cannot, clarify your own understanding before writing the explanation.
