# Classic Style: The Peer-Pointing Mental Model for Explanatory Writing

Pinker's *The Sense of Style* (2014) identifies "classic style" as the prose register best suited to explanation. The key move is a shift in the writer's mental model of the writing relationship: the writer is not a teacher, an authority, or a bureaucrat — the writer is a peer who has noticed something interesting in the world and is turning a companion's attention toward it. "Look at this. See what I see?"

This is not about condescension in reverse. It is about the implied relationship that governs every sentence-level decision — what you explain, what you assume, how much you hedge, whether you say "it has been argued by some researchers that" or "here is what happens."

## The Four Registers — and Why Classic Style Wins for Explanation

Pinker distinguishes several prose styles:

| Style | Writer stance | Effect on the reader |
|-------|--------------|---------------------|
| **Classic** | Peer pointing at reality | Feels like being shown something real; trust is earned by clarity |
| **Practical** | Expert informing a novice | Serviceable but low engagement; implies hierarchy |
| **Self-conscious** | Writer performing expertise | Reader reads the writer, not the subject; distance and hedging accumulate |
| **Postmodern/academic** | Writer demonstrating complexity | Opacity becomes a signal of sophistication; the reader is excluded |

For explanatory writing — technical articles, blog posts, how-things-work essays — classic style produces the best outcomes because it keeps attention on the subject, not on the writer's credentials or caution.

## The Core Principle: Point at the World, Not at Your Uncertainty

In classic style, the writer has done the work of forming a view and is now conveying that view. Hedges and qualifications exist for true uncertainty, not as defensive posturing. The writer trusts that the reader is intelligent enough to understand nuance without a verbal cushion before every claim.

The practical implication: strip the throat-clearing. "It is worth noting that," "one might argue that," "as we have seen above" — these are signals the writer is not yet in classic style. They point at the writer's process, not at the subject.

Replace: "It seems that one potential approach could be to consider the possibility that X."
With: "X works here because Y."

The confidence is not arrogance — it is the posture of someone who has already resolved the uncertainty and is now sharing the resolution.

## The "Peer" Constraint — What It Rules Out

Calling the reader a peer does not mean assuming equivalent expertise. It means:
- You do not talk down to the reader (assume they can follow a logical chain if you give them the rungs)
- You do not perform expertise for the reader (drop jargon to signal that you belong to the club)
- You do assume the reader is curious and capable of updating their model

The peer posture produces a specific diagnostic: if you catch yourself writing in a way that would embarrass you in conversation with a smart, curious colleague, you have left classic style. Write the explanation you would give to that colleague over a whiteboard — then edit for prose rather than speech.

## The Relevance to Curse of Knowledge

Classic style and the curse of knowledge are directly linked. The curse produces prose that is implicitly teacher-to-novice but without the teacher's patience — the expert assumes the reader has the knowledge the expert has, then is surprised when the explanation fails. Classic style is the antidote because it forces a specific reader model (a peer with curiosity but not your specific expertise) and requires the writer to actually show the thing rather than name it.

## How to apply

1. **Set the mental model before drafting:** imagine a specific intelligent peer who has not specialized in this area. Write the explanation you would give that person at a whiteboard — where you would pause, what you would draw, what you would assume they know. Then translate that into prose.
2. **Audit for performer markers:** search for phrases like "it is important to note," "as one might expect," "it can be seen that," "clearly." Delete most of them. If the point is important, state it directly. If it is clear, trust the reader to see it without the label.
3. **Audit for hedging accumulation:** read each paragraph and count the qualifications. If there are more than two hedges per paragraph, ask whether each is tracking genuine uncertainty. If it is defensive, cut it.
4. **Replace naming with showing:** for every abstract claim, ask "can I show an example of this before I name the pattern?" If yes, reorder: example first, then the abstraction that names it.
5. **The whiteboard test:** read each explanatory passage aloud as if delivering it to a smart colleague who has five minutes to understand the core idea. Where you fumble or feel the need to rephrase, revise — the prose has lost the peer posture.
