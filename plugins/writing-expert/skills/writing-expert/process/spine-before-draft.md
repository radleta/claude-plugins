# The Spine: Structure as a Written Pass

John McPhee describes in *Draft No. 4* what he calls the structural pass: after reporting but before prose drafting, write down the sequence of the piece. Not a heading hierarchy, but a plain-language list of moves — what this section does, what that section does, and why they are in that order.

McPhee spent months on structure before writing a word of prose for complex pieces. The structural document is the "spine" — the skeleton the flesh attaches to. It is itself a writing product, not a preliminary thought.

The key insight is that structural decisions are harder than sentence decisions and require a different cognitive mode. Deciding "this example comes before that argument because it gives the reader the evidence they need to accept the claim" is architectural work. Doing architectural thinking while also crafting sentences causes each to contaminate the other. The spine pass isolates structure so the draft pass can focus on prose.

For most pieces this does not need to be elaborate. A blog post spine might be five bullets. A technical article might be eight sections with one-sentence summaries of what each must accomplish. The test of a good spine: someone reading only the spine should understand what the final piece will argue and in what order.

**Source:** John McPhee, *Draft No. 4* (structure chapters; months-on-structure anecdote; "the greasy pole" draft process).

## How to apply

1. **Write the spine in a separate document or section, not inside the draft file.** Keep it visible during drafting — the draft answers to the spine.
2. **For each spine entry, write a one-sentence job description:** what must this section accomplish for the reader to be ready for the next section?
3. **Test sequence by asking: if I swapped sections 2 and 3, would anything break?** If not, at least one of them is structural filler. Rearrange until every move is load-bearing.
4. **The spine is revisable** — if the draft reveals a better sequence, update the spine, then continue drafting against the updated version. The spine is a constraint, not a cage.
5. **Check for the hidden assumption at each joint:** what does the reader need to believe at the end of section N to receive section N+1? If you cannot name it, the joint is weak.
