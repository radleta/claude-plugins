# The Shitty First Draft

Anne Lamott's central process permission in *Bird by Bird*: the first draft is not a product, it is a tool. Its job is to get material out of your head and onto the page so you can see what you actually have. Every published writer has a terrible first draft; the difference is they do not show it.

The key discipline is turning off the inner critic entirely during the drafting pass. Lamott names this voice "radio station KFKD" — it is useful during revision but destructive during drafting because it kills forward motion. You cannot discover what you think while simultaneously judging whether you are allowed to think it.

The draft is the writer's private document. It is not for the reader. Its only obligation is to exist. From it you extract the 10–20% that was real, cut the rest, and revise toward something worth publishing. The surplus is not failure — it is the cost of discovery.

This principle applies equally to: blog posts (start with brain dump, not introduction), technical articles (write out all the context you know, then find the article inside it), and short pieces (even a 400-word post benefits from a 1,200-word first dump).

**Source:** Anne Lamott, *Bird by Bird* (shitty first drafts chapter; KFKD radio metaphor).

## How to apply

1. **Before drafting, set a timer and commit to not backspacing.** If a sentence is wrong, write the next one; do not fix the wrong one. Revision is a different pass.
2. **Write past the threshold of discomfort.** The first 15 minutes produces the critic-filtered surface material. The useful discovery usually starts after that.
3. **Accept a word count 2–4× the target length** as the normal output of a first draft. You will cut; that is the plan.
4. **Do not read the draft immediately.** Let it sit (even 30 minutes). The revision pass requires distance from the drafting state.
5. **Separate permission from quality:** the first-draft question is "did I get it out?" — not "is it good?" Quality is a revision question, not a drafting question.
