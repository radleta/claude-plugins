# Openings: The Contract Principle

The first paragraph is not merely bait — it is a contract. It tells the reader what kind of piece this is, what emotional register to occupy, what they are going to get out of staying. Break that contract mid-piece and the reader feels cheated even if they cannot articulate why.

## What the Opening Must Do

A strong opening does three things simultaneously:

1. **Sets register** — intimate or authoritative, confessional or analytical, skeptical or celebratory. If you open in close third-person and drift to lecture voice by section two, the reader feels the shift as a betrayal.
2. **Establishes specificity** — Lamott's core teaching is that specificity is the writer's only currency. An abstract opening ("Technology is changing how we work") promises nothing. A specific opening lands the reader in a scene or moment already in progress. The reader infers the general from the specific, not the other way around. (*Bird by Bird*, Anne Lamott)
3. **Creates a question the piece will answer** — the opening should plant an itch without announcing it. Not "in this post I will show you three reasons..." but a situation, tension, or detail that makes the reader lean forward.

## The "Already In Progress" Move

Deconstructed from exemplar long-form journalism and technical narrative: strong openings tend to drop the reader into something already in motion. A conversation underway. A decision already being made. A problem already visible. This technique signals that the piece respects the reader's time — it does not warm up.

The inverse — the scenic establishing shot that summarizes context before revealing the actual subject — reads as nervous filler. Cut everything before the first moment that contains real energy.

## The Register Trap

Technical blog posts regularly violate the contract by opening with a disclaimer, a motivating question, or background section before getting to the actual subject. The reader arrives prepared for insight and gets a preamble instead. The contract is broken before it is written.

If your opening paragraph could be deleted without changing anything important, delete it. The real opening is usually the second paragraph.

## How to apply

- **Before drafting:** Write the one-sentence version of what your piece promises — the emotional and intellectual contract. Keep it visible while you draft.
- **After drafting:** Read only your first three paragraphs. Does each sentence advance the energy, or stall it? Cut any sentence that exists to ease you into the subject rather than land the reader in it.
- **Register test:** Paste your opening into a document with no other context. Have someone read it cold — can they tell what kind of piece this is and what they're going to get? If not, the contract is vague.
- **"Already in progress" check:** Does your opening begin before the actual subject arrives? If yes, find the first sentence that contains real energy and cut everything before it.
- **Specificity check:** Replace any abstract noun in your first paragraph with the specific thing it represents. If it reads better, keep the specific thing. (It usually does.)
